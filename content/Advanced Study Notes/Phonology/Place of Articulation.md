## **Columns - Place of Articulation**
- Terms like "BIlabial", "Dental", "Palatal", etc. (within the columns of the chart) are what's called as the **place of articulation.**
- These describe *where* the main point of obstruction or restriction of air from our lungs are to produce different sounds. (Lips = Labial, Teeth = Dental, etc.)

# Bilabial
- "Labial" means lips. "Bilabial" means the two lips (upper and lower) touching together or coming close to each other to obstruct/constrict air. 
- Sounds like /p/, & /b/, are bilabial.

## Labiodental
- Coming from "labial" (labio) & "dental" (meaning teeth). Labiodental consonants are made by your lower lip and upper teeth coming close or touching together.
- Sounds like /f/, & /v/, are labiodental

## Dental 
- "Dental" meaning "to do with teeth". These consonants are made by touching the tip/blade of the tongue with the back of the upper front teeth or protruding the tongue between the upper front teeth.
- Sounds like /$\theta$/ (th in "thorn"), & /$\eth$/ (th in "this") are Dental

## Alveolar 
- the Alveolar Ridge is the boney, bumpy structure just behind the upper front teeth. Alveolar sounds are made with the tip or blade of the tongue touching or approaching the alveolar ridge. 
- Sounds like /n/, & /s/, are Alveolar

## Postalveolar 
- "Postal" coming from post, meaning after. "veolar" still coming from "Alveolar"
- "postalveolar" meaning "After (behind) the Alveolar Ridge." This isn't exactly a region of the mouth, because the Alveolar ridge immediately transitions into the hard palate, but it's a matter of distance.
- This sound is made with the blade of the tongue positioned just behind the alveolar ridge, right where the roof of the mouth begins to curve upward. 
- Sounds like /$\int$/ (sh in "shore") & /$\text{ʒ}$/ (s sound in the middle of "measure") are Postalveolar.

## Retroflex 
- "Retro" as a shortened form of "Retrograde" (meaning to go backwards or in reverse order)
- "Flex" meaning to bend a joint or tense a muscle. 
- "Retroflex" is a description of how the tongue moves while sounding these consonants out. It curls backwards and upwards towards the hard palate and obstructing the air. 
- There are no retroflex phonemes in the English language. (an allophone of /n/ and /ɹ/ (American pronunciation of the letter r) does however exist.)

## Palatal
- "Palatal" refers to the **hard palate**. Just behind the alveolar ridge is the same bony and hard structure but with only a single, dividing ridge in the middle. 
- Made with the body (front) of the tongue raised against the hard palate.
- The difference between this & retroflex is the tongue does not bend to meet the hard palate in palatal consonants. It simply raises and expands. 
- /j/ (y in "yes") is a Palatal consonant sound.

## Velar 
- "Velar" coming from the word "Velum", the Velum, otherwise known as the "Soft palate" is right behind the hard palate and is... well... soft. It's the component that directs airflow from the lungs to either the nose or the mouth. It has the ability to drop and meet the back of the tongue, while also acting as a passive articulator in some consonant sounds. 
- Velar sounds are made with the back of the tongue touching or approaching the velum. 
- Sounds like /g/, & /ŋ/ (ng in "thing") are Velar. 

## Uvular
- Made with the back of the tongue touching or approaching the uvula—the small, fleshy mass hanging at the back of the soft palate. This is done by retracting and raising the back of the tongue towards the back of the throat. This blocks the pathway to the esophagus, giving the sense that you are about to gag.
- There are no Uvular consonant sounds in standard English. 

## Pharyngeal 
- "Pharyngeal" meaning "to do with the Pharynx". 
- The Pharynx is the area known simply as the "throat". It is an entire hollowed chamber that air from the lungs travel to. It goes up from the top of the neck area up past the back wall of your mouth and into your nasal cavity. 
- In phonetics, it's best to think of the Pharynx as the back wall of the throat, just behind and down from the uvula. Pharyngeal sounds are made by retracting the root of the tongue towards this wall. 
- There are no Pharyngeal cosnonant sounds in standard English. 

## Glottal 
- Made by narrowing or completely closing the vocal folds inside the larynx to create friction or a momentary stop. The **glottis** is the area/opening between the vocal folds.
- Sounds like /h/ are Glottal sounds.
