# **Rows - Manner of Articulation.**
- Terms like "Plosive", "Nasal", "Trill", etc. (within the rows of the chart) are what's called as the **manner of articulation**. 
- These describe *how* the different parts of our mouth obstruct, restrict, or modify the airflow from the lungs to produce different sounds. 

## **Plosive**
- **Plosive consonants** are made by completely blocking the flow of air as it leaves the body, normally followed by releasing the air.
- Meaning you build up air (Try putting your lips together, and breathe out through your mouth as hard as you can), and then release (whether by simply moving your lips apart, or allowing the air pressure to blow through your lips, creating an opening.)  
- **An easy way to remember plosives is to think about the word "explode", or "explosives"**. You are literally allowing air to explode out of your mouth, by obstructing it with (for example: your lips) and building up pressure.
- English pronunciation contains **6 plosive [[phonemes]]:** **/p,b,t,d,k,g/:**

![[Plosives.png]]

-> As a side note, lips aren't the only way to block air out of your mouth and create plosive. A plosive is, again, the **Manner of articulation**. Which body part you use would be the **Place of articulation.** (A consonant produced using your lips will either be Bilabial (/p, b/) or Labiodental (/f, v/)) 

-> Fun fact, a "monolabial" sound (meaning a sound produced with only one lip, not touching any other part of your mouth) is **phonetically impossible**. Try it, lol.

## **Nasal
- **Nasal consonants** are made by lowering the soft palate (velum), allowing air to escape freely through the nose while the mouth is completely blocked. 
- This is somewhat difficult to understand if you don't know where your soft palate is and how it feels when you certain words. But try closing your mouth, put a finger just below your nose, on top of your upper lip, and say /m/ or /η/ (ng), a gentle breeze should blow through your nose onto your finger. 
- This is because when the soft palate lowers, it creates a pathway for air to travel upwards through the nose, instead of through the mouth. 
- English pronunciation contains **3 nasal [[Phonemes]]:** /m/, /n/, /η/

## Trill
- **Trilled consonants** are made by rapidly vibrating a certain organ to produce sound.
- This is done by placing an **active [[Articulators|articulator]]** (the movable structures in the mouth that configure the vocal tract) near **another, either active or passive, articulator** (passive articulators are stationary organs that active articulators move towards.) and vibrating them together with air.
- If you struggle to trill your r's, or blow raspberries, this is pretty difficult to understand and test with your body. 
- English pronunciation contains **0 trill phonemes**, because standard American & British English rely on a softer "R" sound. And the bilabial trill cannot produce any meaning within the English language. 
- This is not to be confused with trills not "existing" within English spaces. Some trills can become [[allophones]] for some English accents. 
- Trills can also serve as what's called an "Extralinguistic Gesture" (Paralanguage), meaning they can express emotion, attitude, or social meaning, but cannot be classified into a sentence, noun, verb, or adjective.
- Trills can also serve as onomatopoeia or ideophones. Words that imitate or simulate sounds heard from real-world circumstances.

## **Tap or Flap**
- Tap or Flap consonants are made by a single, rapid muscular contraction where the tongue briefly strikes the roof of the mouth, usually the alveolar ridge. 
- The only Tap or Flap consonant that exists within the English language is the **Voiced Alveolar Tap/Flap**. This is the sound found in "wa(t)er", sounding like a short /r/ (voiced alveolar trill)
- **So why the difference?** While they sound the same, these 2 phonemes obstruct/constrict airflow in different ways. The trill is characterized by a *passive* kind of vibration, while a Tap/Flap moves the tongue in a singular motion rapidly.

## **Fricatives**
- Fricatives are consonant sounds produced by forcing air through a narrow passage in the vocal tract. This creates a continuous, turbulent stream of air that produces an audible hissing or buzzing noise.
- **Fun Fact:** Every [[Place of Articulation]] has a voiced and voiceless fricative associated with it!

## Lateral Fricative 
- A lateral fricative is a speech sound produced by blocking air in the center of the mouth while allowing it to escape with audible friction (hissing) over one or both sides of the tongue.
- Basically, put your tongue on the alveolar ridge, or even in the postalveolar region, and just breathe like you're saying an H. It's like saying an "L" but instead of just letting air passively go through the sides as you vibrate your vocal chords, you breathe or hiss, like creating an "H" sound. This should somewhat sound like the "sh" sound (voiceless postalveolar fricative), and when voiced, "zh" (voiced postalveolar fricative)
- **Standard English does *not* have any lateral fricatives.***

## **Approximant**
- Approximants are consonant sounds produced by bringing [[Articulators]] close together—like the lips or tongue—without causing the turbulent air friction (hissing) characteristic of fricatives. Positioned acoustically between vowels and consonants, they are smooth, vowel-like, and always voiced.
- Also called "Semi-vowels" because their mouth shapes are exactly the same as a vowel. 
- **So why the difference?** Well, approximants are consonants simply because they cannot form the core of a syllable. Think of any word with a "y" or "w", although sounded out as "e" or "o" in an isolated, elongated pronunciation, these are not considered a syllable of their own. Words like "yes", "wet", "win", "yuck", are all one syllable despite phonetically having two "o" or "e" *shapes*. 
- **So what can we learn from this?** From everything thus far, we know that shape and sound are not a determining factor in a sound's classification. Only the technique in producing that sound is. Whether a sound is a consonant or a vowel, whether a sound is an allophone or a phoneme. It depends on **articulatory mechanics** (how the body moves) and **phonological function** (how the sound behaves in a syllable), rather than just raw acoustic sound.
- **There are 2 Approximant Phonemes in Standard English:** /ɹ/, (soft english r/american r) /j/ (y sound, or spanish j)

## **Lateral Approximant**
- A lateral approximant is a consonant where airflow is blocked in the center of the mouth but escapes freely around one or both sides of the tongue. The most common example is the "L" sound in English, classified phonetically as a voiced alveolar lateral approximant
- As an English speaker, a lot of these can get very confusing. Same with the other manners of articulation. But as complex as they may seem, each of them have their own part to play in the languages of this world.
