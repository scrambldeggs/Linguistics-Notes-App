A consonant, in Phonology, is a speech sound produced by **obstructing or restricting the flow of air** through the mouth using the **lips, teeth, or tongue**

# **Terminology Within the IPA Consonant Chart

## **Pulmonic** 
- Word meaning "Relates to the Lungs". 
- Next to the title of the chart (**Consonants**), "Pulmonic" is written to describe the way English consonants work. 
- Therefore, **English Consonants are pulmonic.**
- When exploring the IPA for other languages, you can find that there are what's called **"Non-Pulmonic Consonants"**, these are **clicks, ejectives, or implosives,** found commonly in African & South American languages.

## **Rows - [[Manner of Articulation]].**
- Terms like "Plosive", "Nasal", "Trill", etc. (within the rows of the chart) are what's called as the **manner of articulation**. 
- These describe *how* the different parts of our mouth obstruct, restrict, or modify the airflow from the lungs to produce different sounds. 

## **Columns - [[Place of Articulation]].** 
- Terms like "BIlabial", "Dental", "Palatal", etc. (within the columns of the chart) are what's called as the **place of articulation.**
- These describe *where* the main point of obstruction or restriction of air from our lungs are to produce different sounds. (Lips = Labial, Teeth = Dental, etc.)

## **Voiced & Voiceless Consonants**
- All consonants in the IPA Chart can be categorized by Voiced or Voiceless.
- Voiced consonants are sounded by vibrating your vocal chords alongside using the technique described by the sound. For example, /b/ (the "b" in ball), is a **voiced bilabial plosive**. Notice how it incorporates the vibration of your vocal chords! 
- Voiceless consonants are the exact opposite. You can make these sounds without vibrating your vocal chords. For example, if you take away the voicing in /b/, it becomes /p/ (the "p" in pan")
- Studying this requires some experimentation and truly feeling your body. If you have a hard time feeling your throat/vocal chords, try putting you hand on your larynx to feel the vibration when sounding a consonant. If it vibrates, you are pronouncing a Voiced consonant. If it does not, it is voiceless. 
- Fun fact: **All nasal phonemes** in the English language are voiced.
