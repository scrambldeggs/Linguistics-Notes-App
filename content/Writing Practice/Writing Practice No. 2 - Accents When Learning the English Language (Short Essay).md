With the rise of the internet granting open access to large amounts of information, language learning has become increasingly more accessible over the last few decades. As of 2026, it is estimated that 1.5 billion people are learning English daily all over the world. 

Even then, with all of the information available to us, one of the most difficult hurdles to overcome in learning English is the accent. Due to its reach, there are hundreds, if not thousands of ways to pronounce each word in the English language, and this impacts how the language is learned on a global scale.

A common scenario is learning English as your second language. Just the difference between one's primary language and English can stump and demotivate learners from progressing further. According to The Sound, Smell, and Feeling of Burgundy by Francis Wayne, our "voice muscles" (as he describes it) can also develop muscle memory. This muscle memory relates not just to sounding out consonants and vowels, but also in reading certain words and letters, and interpreting the rules of a language.

A good example of this is the "Spanish accent". Characterized by long, clearly pronounced vowels and harsh consonant tones. Spanish is a Latin based language, while English is Germanic. Learning the "English accent" as a Spanish learner is like living in the Arctic when you grew up in the Sahara for 50 years. The muscle memory of a Latin language is very hard not just to unlearn, but compartmentalize so that you can fluently speak two, largely different languages. 

So the next time you see somebody struggling to speak a certain word the "right way", remember that each of us have lived our own lives, have our own stories, and know different rules of communication. This "accent problem" proves that English, and language itself isn't exclusive to a certain ruleset, but is dynamic and adaptable to anyone who is willing to learn it.
